Three models, one driving force

Chemical bonding reflects changes in the total energy of electrons and nuclei. “Transferred,” “shared,” and “delocalized” electrons are useful models, not rigid categories. Real bonding often lies on a continuum.

ModelElectron descriptionTypical properties
IonicElectron density concentrated around ions in an extended electrostatic latticeHigh melting points; brittle; conduct when molten or aqueous, if mobile ions are present
CovalentElectron density shared between nuclei; may be localized or delocalizedDiscrete molecules or giant networks; properties depend on structure and intermolecular forces
MetallicElectrons delocalized across an extended solidElectrical/thermal conduction; malleability; ductility

These are trends, not absolute rules. Diamond is a giant covalent solid, graphite has conductive layers, and ionic compounds can have appreciable covalent character.

Module 01

Ionic bonding

Electrostatic attraction holds positive and negative ions in a three-dimensional crystal. We write empirical formula units, not separate NaCl molecules, for typical ionic solids.

Core ideas

Metals often form cations by losing valence electrons, while nonmetals often form anions by gaining them. The resulting crystal maximizes favorable attractions while limiting repulsions.

Formula ratios: total positive charge must balance total negative charge.

Properties: ions are fixed in solid crystals, but can transport charge when mobile in melts or solutions. A shifted lattice can bring like charges into contact, promoting fracture.

Beyond the basics

Electrostatic potential energy for a pair is approximately E ∝ q₁q₂/r. Actual lattice enthalpy also depends on crystal geometry, repulsion, and polarization.

Born–Haber cycles use Hess's law to connect atomization, ionization, electron affinity, bond dissociation, and lattice formation energies.

Fajans' rules: small, highly charged cations and large, polarizable anions increase covalent character.

How to determine ionic charges and write formulas

  1. Identify each ion. Main-group metals commonly lose electrons: group 1 forms +1; group 2 forms +2; aluminum forms +3. Nonmetals commonly gain electrons: group 17 forms −1; group 16 forms −2; group 15 forms −3. Transition-metal charges may vary, so use the Roman numeral or other chemical information.
  2. Write the ions with their charges. For magnesium nitride, write Mg²⁺ and N³⁻, not Mg and N without charges.
  3. Find the smallest neutral ratio. The least common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6: three Mg²⁺ ions provide +6; two N³⁻ ions provide −6.
  4. Write the formula, cation first. Mg₃N₂. Use subscripts for ion counts and omit the subscript 1.
  5. Check and simplify. Total charge must be zero; reduce any shared factor in subscripts. Do not put ionic charges in the final neutral formula.

Worked example: calcium chloride

Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ → one Ca²⁺ plus two Cl⁻ → CaCl₂. Check: (+2) + 2(−1) = 0.

Worked example: aluminum oxide

Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → 2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0 → Al₂O₃. A 3:2 ratio would not be neutral.

Further examples: potassium sulfide K₂S; calcium nitride Ca₃N₂; magnesium oxide MgO (not Mg₂O₂). For polyatomic ions, keep the ion intact and use parentheses when more than one is needed, e.g. Ca(NO₃)₂.

Worked example: Al₂O₃ charge balance

Al forms Al³⁺ and O forms O²⁻. Two Al³⁺ contribute +6 and three O²⁻ contribute −6. The minimum neutral ratio is 2:3, giving Al₂O₃. Do not use 3:2 simply because the charge numbers appear in the opposite order without verifying neutrality.

Extension: Why a more exothermic lattice does not guarantee solubility

For dissolving an ionic solid, breaking the lattice costs energy while hydrating ions releases energy. Entropy also matters, so dissolution is determined by Gibbs free energy, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, rather than lattice enthalpy alone.

Module 02

Covalent bonding

Atoms lower their energy through constructive overlap of orbitals and increased electron density between nuclei. Lewis diagrams provide bookkeeping; VSEPR predicts shapes; orbital and molecular-orbital models explain details Lewis structures miss.

Core ideas

Lewis structures: count valence electrons, connect atoms, fill terminal octets, complete central atoms, and check formal charges. Hydrogen typically forms one bond; second-period elements generally obey an octet.

Bond order: single, double, and triple bonds contain one, two, and three shared electron pairs. Greater bond order often implies shorter, stronger bonds for comparable atoms.

VSEPR: count electron domains around the central atom. Lone pairs influence angle and molecular geometry.

College-level extensions

Formal charge: FC = valence e⁻ − nonbonding e⁻ − ½(bonding e⁻). Resonance describes a delocalized electronic structure, not rapidly alternating distinct molecules.

σ and π bonds: a single bond is σ; a double bond has one σ + one π; a triple bond has one σ + two π.

Orbital models: sp, sp², and sp³ hybridization are useful local bonding descriptions. Molecular orbital theory introduces bonding/antibonding orbitals and can explain O₂ paramagnetism.

How to draw Lewis structures: step by step

  1. Count available valence electrons. Sum the valence electrons from every atom. Add one for each negative charge; subtract one for each positive charge.
  2. Choose a central atom and sketch single bonds. Hydrogen is terminal. Usually the less electronegative non-hydrogen atom is central. Each single bond uses two electrons.
  3. Complete terminal-atom octets first. Add lone pairs; hydrogen needs only two electrons (a duet).
  4. Place any remaining electrons on the central atom. Check whether it has a full octet (except H, Be/B exceptions and relevant expanded-octet cases).
  5. Form multiple bonds if needed. Move a terminal lone pair into a second or third bond to complete the central octet without changing the electron total.
  6. Verify the electron count and formal charges. FC = valence e⁻ − lone-pair e⁻ − bonding lines (each line counts as one toward each atom). Minimize unnecessary formal charge and place negative charge preferentially on more electronegative atoms when sensible.
  7. Check resonance, brackets, and geometry. Put ions in brackets with the total charge. When multiple equivalent contributors exist, draw resonance forms and explain delocalization. Use VSEPR to predict 3D shape.

Worked example: H₂O

6 + 2(1) = 8 e⁻. Two O–H single bonds use 4 e⁻. Place the other 4 as two lone pairs on O. O has two bonding pairs and two lone pairs, so the molecular shape is bent.

Worked example: CO₂

4 + 2(6) = 16 e⁻. Begin O–C–O (4 e⁻). Give both O atoms octets (12 more e⁻). Carbon lacks an octet, so make two C=O bonds: O=C=O, with two lone pairs on each O.

Worked example: NH₃

5 + 3(1) = 8 e⁻. Three N–H bonds use 6 e⁻; the remaining pair belongs on N. Three bonding domains plus one lone pair give a trigonal-pyramidal molecular shape.

HL extension: NO₃⁻

5 + 3(6) + 1 = 24 e⁻. Start with N bonded to three O atoms, fill O octets, then form one N=O double bond. Formal charges are N +1, two single-bonded O −1 each, and double-bonded O 0. Draw three equivalent resonance contributors in brackets, overall −1.

Interactive 4 · Bond polarity versus molecular polarity

Change the electronegativity difference. Observe that bond polarity and overall molecular dipole are different questions.

EN bands are qualitative, not precise universal boundaries. Geometry and bond-dipole vectors determine net polarity; this visualization assumes two bonds of equal magnitude.

Worked example: nitrate, NO₃⁻

Total valence electrons: 5 + 3(6) + 1 = 24. Three resonance contributors are possible, with one N=O double bond in each. The π electron density is delocalized, so all N–O bonds are equivalent (average bond order about 1⅓).

Intermolecular forces are not covalent bonds

London dispersion interactions occur in all particles; permanent dipole–dipole interactions occur in polar molecules; hydrogen bonding is an especially strong directional interaction when H bonded to N, O, or F interacts with a suitable lone pair. Boiling molecular liquids generally separates molecules without breaking their internal covalent bonds.

Module 03

Metallic bonding

A metallic crystal has positively charged ion cores and electrons in extended electronic states. Delocalization explains electrical transport, while nondirectional cohesion helps layers shift without immediate fracture.

Core ideas

Metallic bonding is attraction between positive ion cores and delocalized electrons. Conductivity comes from electron motion under an electric field, not from mobile metal ions in the solid.

Malleability: layers can move while the extended bonding remains. Ductility: metal can be drawn into wires. Metallic properties also depend on crystal defects and grain structure.

College-level extensions

Band theory: atomic orbitals combine into bands. Metals have accessible states close in energy, allowing charge carriers to respond to fields.

Temperature and resistivity: rising lattice vibrations usually increase electron scattering in pure metals.

Alloys: substitutional and interstitial atoms can hinder dislocation motion. Hardness cannot be predicted by simple valence electron counting alone.

Interactive 6 · Pure metal versus alloy

Worked example: conductivity in copper versus molten NaCl

In a copper wire, delocalized electrons transport charge while the positive ion cores remain at lattice sites. In molten NaCl, freely moving Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions carry current. The charge carriers are different even though both materials conduct.

Extension: band gaps and semiconductors

A simplified metal has partially occupied bands or overlapping bands. A semiconductor has a smaller band gap, so temperature, doping, and excitation can create mobile electrons and holes. An insulator generally has a larger gap. Band structure refines the electron-sea model.
